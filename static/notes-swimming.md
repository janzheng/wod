# Swimming — Technique Notes

Freestyle, technique only. Not speed, not distance. The poolside pages are in this program — **Full Session** is the default. This is the reasoning behind them.

---

## The one problem we're solving

**You were swimming flat.** Almost no body rotation.

That single thing explains everything you reported: shoulders giving out (the arms were supplying all the propulsion), legs contributing nothing (a kick can't connect to a flat body, so "kick harder" never fixes it), and general early fatigue.

**The tell was your breathing sides.** Left easy, right hard. In freestyle you breathe *by rolling the body*, not by turning the head — and a head-turner can crank the neck far enough on their good side to get away with it. The weak side is where the habit shows. So the right-side difficulty was never a right-side problem.

**Heads up about your own strength:** strong lats let you muscle through bad position instead of fixing it. The stronger you pull, the longer you can paper over it. That's why we're not touching catch or pull work yet — it would just make the papering-over more effective.

---

## Not sinking during the side-kick drill

The most common question, and the honest answer is that **your arms were never holding you up** — not in this drill, and not in normal freestyle either. Arms pull you forward. They don't keep you at the surface.

What keeps you up is your chest. Your lungs are a balloon up front; your legs are dense and want to hang.

<details open>
<summary><strong>The four fixes, in order of how much they matter</strong></summary>

1. **Lean on your sternum.** Press the chest a few degrees down toward the bottom. The body seesaws over your lungs and the legs rise. This feels backwards — you press *down* to get your legs *up* — and it's the one that does the real work.
2. **The bottom arm is a balance lever, not a paddle.** Reach it long and slightly **down**, not up toward the surface. It shifts you forward over the float. Letting it drift up and shorten is what drops the legs.
3. **Point your toes.** A flexed foot is a brake — it kills lift and adds drag at the same time.
4. **If it still sinks, cut the distance.** Half a length in good position, stand up, reset. A wobbling full length just grooves the wobble.

</details>

**Feel it on your back first.** Kicking on your back — head back, ears under, water at your goggle line — is the fastest way to prove the float to yourself: your face is out, there's no breathing to manage, and you can feel that your lungs are what hold you up and that keeping a little air in them helps. Once you believe it there, the side-kick stops being a drill you might sink in. **The fault that ruins it is tucking your chin to look at your feet** — hips drop, and then you kick hard just to stay up.

**Two honest notes.** Your legs genuinely do sink more than average — muscular, dense, not much fat down there. That's real, not bad form; it just means the chest cue matters more for you than for most people. And the standard coaching answer to this question is fins. You don't need them in a 25m pool, but it's worth knowing that most people you've seen do this drill smoothly had help.

## Breathing: water in the mouth

**Water in the mouth is a LATE roll, not a small one.**

Your head drags a bow wave, and there's a trough beside it. **Your mouth breathes in that trough** — below the level of the flat surface. Arrive at the breath on time and there's an air pocket waiting. Arrive late and the window's gone; the mouth comes up over the wave instead of into the hole.

<details open>
<summary><strong>The fixes</strong></summary>

- **Half your mouth stays in the water, and you sip air from the top corner.** This is standard freestyle breathing, not a compromise or a beginner version: one goggle in and one out, one corner of your mouth in and one out. Trying to get your whole mouth clear means lifting your head — and your head going up is what drives your legs down. **Blow all your air out underwater so the inhale is a quick sip.** Most water in the mouth comes from trying to take a long, slow breath.
- **Keep your lower goggle in the water.** If both eyes clear the surface you've left the trough. It feels like you can't possibly get air down there. You can — that's where the air is.
- **Roll on every stroke, not just the breathing ones.** If you arrive already on your side, the breath is nearly free. If you arrive flat and *then* heave the shoulder up, it's expensive and it's late.
- **On 6-3-6, breathe during the 6 kicks, not the 3 strokes.** You're fully on your side there, so that breath costs nothing. That exact sensation is the thing to smuggle into real swimming.
- **Lead arm long and patient.** On a right-side breath that's your **left** arm out front. Press it down early and the shoulder drops and your mouth goes under.

</details>

**Rotation costing you effort right now is expected.** It's a new pattern you're running consciously. It gets cheap when it gets continuous — the effort is not a sign you're doing it wrong.

## Kicking: what's supposed to move

**The kick starts at the hip. Everything below it is loose.** Think of your leg as a whip: the top of the thigh starts the motion, and the knee and ankle just follow.

**The hip is the joint at the very top of your leg**, where the thigh bone sits in the pelvis. "From the hip" means the whole leg swings from there in one piece, like walking. The muscles are the walking ones: hip flexors in front (the spot the couch stretch pulls on), glutes behind (the RDL muscles). Not the same as the **pelvis**, the bowl of bone the two hips sit on either side of: when a cue says "let the hips lead the shoulders," that's the pelvis turning with your body.

**To feel it on dry land:** hold a wall, stand on one leg and swing the other forward and back, knee straight but not locked, like a pendulum. That's the kick. In the pool it's the same motion lying down, much smaller and lighter.

<details open>
<summary><strong>The kick, piece by piece</strong></summary>

- **Hip: the only part that works.** Hip flexors start the down-kick, glutes bring the leg back up. Both lightly.
- **Knee: along for the ride.** It bends a little on its own as the thigh starts down, then straightens at the end like the tip of a whip. If you're *pulling* your heels toward your butt, the knee is driving. That's the bicycle kick, and it's what burns your thighs.
- **Ankle: floppy.** Toes pointed, big toes almost brushing. A loose foot works like a flipper; a stiff or flexed one is a brake.
- **Size: small.** Feet no more than about a foot apart. On the way up, heels just break the surface: a quiet boil, not a splash.

</details>

**In easy freestyle the kick isn't your engine.** It keeps your legs up and in time with the roll; it barely pushes you forward. Your legs are your biggest muscles, so a big kick burns through your air for almost nothing, and that's the out-of-breath-like-you-sprinted feeling. **The right kick feels like too little.**

**Don't train it to be stronger.** Same trap as the lats: fitness would just let you keep the expensive version. The Kick Drills are for feel — the wall first, then on your back where breathing is free.

## The one number

**Stroke count on one length** — early in the session and late, at the same easy effort. That's it.

It measures efficiency rather than effort, and it goes **down** as you improve. Laps and times are not tracked here and are never owed.

You've already described a lower stroke count without counting one: "gliding forward more" and "going faster on less energy spent going forward" both mean more distance per stroke. The number just confirms what you felt.

## Standing rules

- **Drills go first, while you're fresh.** A drill done tired grooves the bad version.
- **Slow is correct on drills.** If you're breathing hard at a slow pace, the kick is too big — shrink it before anything else.
- **Watch the right shoulder, not the volume.** Freestyle recovery is a big overhead arc and that shoulder has history. Volume is cheap; a crabby shoulder is the signal to stop.
- **Swim near pull day, not legs.** Swimming is lat and shoulder work — it stacks with pull and barely touches legs.
- **Kicking wants pointed toes; your squat limiter is the opposite direction of that same ankle.** Different range. Swimming isn't secretly fixing your squat.
