## The argument

A movement-*selection* list, not a program (no sets/reps). The thesis: pick a small set of durable compound + isolation movements you can keep doing — and progressively overload — for life. It's a "longevity strength menu," and the spirit ("repeat until you die") maps directly onto the program's own "build decades, not weeks" framing.

So the list is mostly **validation** that the program's exercise selection already sits on the durable, do-it-for-decades end.

**Where it diverges from how we train:**

1. **It's framed as "get stronger forever" (progressive overload).** The program is form-first / fuzzy-progression — the floor rises ("hard becomes easy"), we don't chase the number or track per-lift. User confirmed (2026-06-14): "i like fuzzy progression its easier to keep track of." Take the *menu*, leave the overload *mindset*.
2. **#4 standing barbell military press stays OFF.** That bilateral overhead bar press is exactly what the KB single-arm press exists to replace given the right shoulder. The KB strict-press floor has risen (35/40/45, both sides stable), so that's the confirmed overhead path — don't let a "best exercises" list talk us back onto the bar.
3. **He omits the pull-up entirely** — a real miss for a "do until you die" list, and a place where *our* program is better. We keep weighted/bodyweight pull-ups.

**On dips (user's question — "we've been omitting dips, which is confusing"):** Dips were never deliberately banned; they just never got slotted in (the program used the chest-press machine as the most shoulder-controlled horizontal push). The implicit reason to be cautious: dips put the front shoulder in a deep *loaded stretch* at the bottom — the program's at-risk spot. **Resolved W17:** dips swapped IN for chest press, chest-bias / lean-forward version, with the assisted-dip machine as the shoulder-safe fallback. W18 they earn spotlight cues once settled.

## Application to the program

- **Confirmed paths:** fuzzy progression stays; heavy KB single-arm presses stay (barbell military press stays off); dips are in (W17); pull-ups stay.
- **The one genuinely new idea worth adding — trap bar deadlift.** It's the pattern the program lacks under real load: a centered, back-friendly loaded hinge (incline RDL is light/tempo; leg curl is isolation). User has a hex/trap bar, is open to it, but (a) doesn't know the movement, (b) finds plate-loading annoying (a real convenience cost). **Plan:** W18+ legs candidate; introduce LIGHT given the QL history (it's the most QL-friendly loaded hinge but still a loaded deadlift); if added, it becomes the loaded hinge while incline RDL stays the tempo/mobility hinge. Not pushed — user pulls progression.

### How to use a trap/hex bar (for the user)

1. **Step inside the frame** so you're standing in the middle, feet hip-width.
2. **Hinge down and grab the neutral handles** at your sides (palms facing your legs) — this is the whole point of the trap bar: the load sits *around* you, not out in front.
3. **Set the back flat, chest up, hips back** — shoulders slightly ahead of or over the handles, slack pulled out of the bar.
4. **Drive through the whole foot and stand tall** — push the floor away, finish standing upright (don't lean back / overextend at the top).
5. **Hinge back down under control** — hips back first, flat back, lower the plates to the floor.

It's the **most beginner-friendly deadlift** precisely because the centered load means far less lower-back shear than a straight-bar deadlift — which is exactly why it's the right loaded hinge for someone with a QL history. Keep it light; it doesn't need to be heavy to be useful. (Loading faff is real — it loads with standard plates on the sleeves like any barbell; if that's a dealbreaker on a given day, it's optional.)

## Trap bar — the evidence (Stronger by Science)

Source: [Greg Nuckols, "Trap Bar Deadlifts are Underrated"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/trap-bar-deadlifts/) (shared 2026-06-14, user: "trap bar deadlifts is something i could get behind").

Nuckols' thesis: the trap bar is the **better default deadlift for most lifters in most contexts** — more flexible, higher velocity/power output, safer, easier to learn. The similarities to a straight-bar deadlift dominate (same hinge pattern, comparable loads, similar ROM, similar muscle activation); the differences are matters of degree:

- **It IS a real hinge, not a disguised squat.** Hip:knee moment ratio — conventional DL **3.68:1**, trap bar **1.78:1**, back squat **0.83:1**. So the trap bar sits firmly on the hinge side of the continuum, just a touch squattier than a conventional pull. This is what makes it close the program's *loaded-hinge* gap (incline RDL is light/tempo; squat/leg press cover the knee-dominant side).
- **Lower spinal load.** Peak spine and hip moments run lower than the conventional deadlift; peak *knee* moment runs higher (a bit more quad). Lower lumbar stress = the right call given the QL history.
- **Easier to learn + more power.** Most people lift a bit more on a trap bar and can move it faster — better power expression, fewer technique failure modes.

**Net:** the evidence confirms the plan. Trap bar (not conventional barbell DL) is the loaded hinge to introduce — light, on legs day, as incline RDL's heavier complement.
